Night falls on the farm. At the far end of the yard, the old well breathes like a sleeping animal.

You haven’t slept here in twenty-three years. This morning you buried your grandmother Odile in a fine rain, in front of a handful of neighbours who stared at you the way people stare at someone back from another country. Tomorrow, the notary. The day after, the buyers. The Combes farm will be sold before the end of the month, and with it the kitchen that smells of wax, the leaning barn, the walnut tree in the yard. And the well.

The well nobody has opened since the summer of 2003. Since Paul.

Your little brother was seven. You were ten. One August morning his bed was empty and his sandals were set side by side on the rim of the well, neat and straight, as if left outside a door. The gendarmes dragged the water for three days. They found nothing. Odile had a lid put on, oak banded with iron, with a padlock the size of a fist, and she never said Paul’s name in front of you again.

Tonight the lid is leaning against the rim. The padlock hangs open.

You stand on the doorstep for a long time. The wind makes the weathervane creak. Somewhere in the dark, water drips, very far down, as regular as a clock.

On the workbench, under the lean-to of the workshop, a lantern is waiting. Someone has filled it with paraffin. The box of matches lies beside it, half open, like an invitation.
