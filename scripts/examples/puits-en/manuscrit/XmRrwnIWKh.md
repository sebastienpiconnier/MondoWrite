You raise the lantern above your head, and you say:

“**OURDAS**.”

The flame answers the name. It grows all at once, white, immense, and the light bursts through the glass as if someone had just opened a door onto a summer afternoon. The sky of water lights up from below. The thing draws back. For the first time in its very long existence, it has a shadow, and it doesn’t know what to do with it.

“You can’t,” it says, in Paul’s voice.

“Yes,” Paul answers, in his own. “We can.”

He is holding your hand so tight his nails dig into your palm.

The water collapses, comes apart, falls back into the lake in a silent rain. The little lights rise, one by one, set free. Marcel passes very close and whispers in your ear: “Tell her I don’t hold it against her. Tell it to her grave.” Then he goes with the others.

Paul stays.

You carry him on your back to the ladder. You count. He counts with you. At twenty-three, you step over together. Around thirty, as you pass the niche, he tells you to keep the marble after all, “because you’re grown up now, it’s true”.

At forty, the dawn.

The sky is pink above the walnut tree. The birds are starting. You set Paul down on the rim, and he looks at the yard, the house, the workshop, with the seriousness of an inspector.

“Where’s Granny?”

You will have to explain. Everything. The twenty-three years, the grave in the rain, the world that has changed, phones, you growing up without him. It will take a long time. There will be papers, doctors, people who won’t believe you.

None of it matters.

At nine o’clock, you call the notary. You tell him the farm is no longer for sale.

Then you warm some milk in Odile’s kitchen, while Paul, sitting on the rim of the well, puts on his sandals.

**THE END.** *It is the only ending where everyone comes home.*
