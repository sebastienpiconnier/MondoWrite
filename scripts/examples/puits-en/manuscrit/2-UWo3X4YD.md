At the end of the field of grey wheat there is a lake. Black, smooth, immense. It must be the same water as at the bottom of the well, seen from the other side.

Paul is sitting at the edge.

He hasn’t changed. Of course he hasn’t. The striped pyjamas, the scraped knees, the cowlick on the top of his head that no comb could tame. He is throwing pebbles into the water, one by one. They sink without a sound, without a ripple.

You say his name. Your voice breaks halfway through.

He turns round. He looks you up and down, politely, with the calm suspicion children have for a grown-up they don’t know.

“Who are you? You’re not from the house. At the house there’s Granny, there’s me, and there’s the other one.”

*The other one.* That’s what he called you when he was cross.

“The other one is ten,” he adds. “You’re too old. Too big, I mean. Anyway, you’re too grown-up.” He shrugs and picks up another pebble. “I’m waiting for them. Someone told me they’d come.”

Behind him, the surface of the lake has just moved. For the first time.
